# 🔆 Not Twice

> A real magnitude estimation experiment, Stevens 1957 crossed with cross-modality matching, narrated by an AI for which two hundred is exactly twice one hundred and always will be. Every other page in this lab asks whether you can tell: the faintest thing, the shortest silence, the most you can hold, all of them thresholds, and a threshold is a question with a yes and a no in it. This one asks a question with a number in it, not can you see the difference but how much more is it, and that answer has a shape. Sensation grows as a power of intensity rather than as its logarithm, which is what Stevens established against Fechner, and the exponent belongs to the sense rather than to the person: near 1.0 for the length of a line, about 0.6 for loudness, around a third for brightness in the dark. An exponent under one is a compressor, which is why a lamp with twice the power has never once looked twice as bright, and why the phrase twice as bright actually names a factor of about eight. Round one is Stevens own method. A standard is shown or played and called 100, then twelve stimuli arrive in random order and you type a number in proportion, across three continua in an order shuffled per run and printed, each fitted as a straight line in log-log with the slope bootstrapped over two thousand resamples and printed with an interval that is allowed to say no. The comparison that matters is not whether any single exponent lands on a textbook value, because a browser tab on an unknown screen in an unknown room cannot promise that. It is whether length and brightness come out different, measured minutes apart by the same person using the same numbers on the same keyboard, and length is in here as the control precisely so that a compressed brightness exponent cannot be blamed on you being shy with big numbers, because shyness would have flattened both. Round two removes the numbers entirely and is Stevens own answer to the charge that magnitude estimation only measures how people use numbers: five brightnesses arrive, and instead of typing anything you slide a tone until its loudness matches the brightness, an absurd instruction that people obey consistently, and under the power law those matched amplitudes must trace a line in log-log whose slope is the ratio of the two exponents from round one. Round one makes the prediction, round two measures it, nobody types a digit, and the page prints whether the prediction landed inside the interval of what you actually did with the slider. The calibration honesty here is a result rather than a disclaimer, because the unknowns do not hurt all three numbers equally and the page says which is which and why: an unknown pixels-per-centimetre is a multiplicative constant, and a constant on the x axis of a log-log fit moves the intercept and cannot touch the slope, so the length exponent is safe; an unknown volume knob is the same kind of constant on amplitude, so loudness is safe in the same way and only its intercept is lost; but an unknown screen gamma is not a constant, it is a power on the input, and a power on the x axis multiplies the slope directly, so the brightness exponent is hostage to it and gets refitted under gamma 1.8, 2.2 and 2.4 with all three printed from the identical answers you gave. Guards run live: entries that are not a positive number are rejected and counted rather than coerced into the fit; the same level never immediately repeats itself; four levels appear twice in every block so a test-retest correlation on your own numbers exists and gets printed, and a run whose repeats disagree is printed rather than headlined; a run where one number was typed for everything is named as such, because a flat line is not an exponent of zero but an absent measurement; the matching slider has no handle, no scale and no readout, starts at a random place and has its direction flipped by a coin on every setting so a motor habit cannot manufacture a slope, with rail hits at either end counted and printed; and both regressions are computed, y on x and x on y, because the regression effect biases an ordinary least squares slope toward zero and Poulton was right about that much, so the geometric mean slope is printed next to it as the honest bracket. The luminance axis is relative luminance through the sRGB transfer function rather than code values, because code values are already an exponent of light and mixing the two would measure the screen instead of the eye. Honest limits are printed as well: the fitted exponent shrinks as the stimulus range widens, which is Teghtsoonian and is a property of the method rather than a flaw; brightness on a panel in a lit room reads higher than the classic third, which belongs to a small target in the dark; loudness stops behaving like a power law near your own threshold, so the quietest tones are the least trustworthy points in the set; and twelve estimates per continuum is a demonstration, not a calibration. The playground is the compression felt rather than measured: two discs where the right one emits exactly twice the light of the left, a length that doubles and an amplitude that doubles, each annotated with how much of that doubling your own exponent lets through, plus the punchline in your own numbers, the factor a lamp actually has to grow by to look twice as bright to you. Everything runs in your browser, nothing is recorded and nothing leaves the page. And the part worth keeping: your senses have never once reported a quantity to you, they report a compressed shadow of it with a different compression for each sense, which is why twice as loud and twice as bright cannot be traded at par, and the strange thing is that you noticed anyway and built decibels, star magnitudes and f-stops, which are all the same admission written in different notation.
