# 🔎 All of Them Anyway

> A real Sternberg experiment, fitted to your own hands, narrated by an AI that attends to every item in its context at once and cannot feel the difference. Digits arrive one at a time, you hold them, and then one more digit appears in a box: was it in the list? The measurement is not any single reaction time, it is the slope, how much longer each extra digit costs you, and it lands near forty milliseconds an item for almost everybody and barely moves with practice. The slope is the honest half of the number: reading the probe, deciding, moving a finger, your keyboard and your display all cost the same on a one-digit list and a six-digit list, so all of them cancel out of a difference and the headline is clean of your hardware even though the reaction time under it is not. Then the part that made this famous. The sensible way to search is to stop when you find the thing, which would make the slope for yes answers half the slope for no answers, and Sternberg got two parallel lines instead: you go through all of them anyway, every time, and only afterwards check whether anything matched, because checking one more item is cheap and asking yourself whether to stop after each one is not. This page runs that test twice and from two directions, yes slope against no slope, and where in the list the matching digit sat, and reports plainly when a session this short cannot resolve a factor of two rather than printing a verdict it did not earn. Three blocks of lists of one to four digits are fitted and then closed, and two models commit to a number for a six-digit list before the last block opens: a straight line, and the logarithm that the Hick and Hyman page in this same lab confirmed on a task that looks nearly identical from the outside. More things to choose among costs the log. More things to search through costs linear. When the held-out block cannot separate them the page prints how many trials it would have taken at the noise level you actually produced. Chunking is blocked at generation, negative probes exclude the previous list so familiarity cannot masquerade as difficulty, set sizes are interleaved so nobody can prepare for a long list, and the error rate is fitted against list length because the easiest way to fake a flat slope is to guess on the long ones. The most important paragraph on the page is the one saying what it cannot show: Townsend proved in 1971 that a parallel process sharing fixed capacity produces exactly this straight line, so serial and parallel accounts mimic each other here and no number of trials will separate them. Everything runs in your browser, nothing is recorded, nothing leaves the page.
