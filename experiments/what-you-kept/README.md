# 🗃️ What You Kept

> A real visual working memory experiment, Luck and Vogel 1997 crossed with Zhang and Luck 2008, narrated by an AI that has no such limit and no idea what it is missing. It is the sequel to this lab's iconic memory page: there, a flashed grid is almost entirely available for a fraction of a second and then drains away; here the question is what is still in your hands a second later, and the answer is about three or four things. Round one is single-probe change detection at two, four, six and eight coloured squares, flashed for a fifth of a second, hidden for nine hundred milliseconds, then one square returns and you say whether it changed. Capacity comes out as Cowan's K = N x (hit rate minus false alarm rate), and the false alarm term is the whole defence: change and no-change trials are exactly 50/50 within every set size, so answering changed every time scores a capacity of exactly zero and so does answering same every time. The interesting test is not whether K beats zero but whether it is FLAT, because a fixed number of slots predicts the same K at four items and at eight, and that difference is bootstrapped over two thousand resamples and printed with an interval that is allowed to say no. Round two is the harder one and turns a demonstration into an argument: instead of asking whether a colour changed it asks WHICH colour it was, on a continuous wheel, so every trial yields an error in degrees rather than a bit. Those errors are fitted as a mixture of a von Mises bell around the truth and a flat carpet of guessing, at three items and at six, and then twice more under the two rival accounts, each with exactly three free parameters so the comparison is a straight likelihood ratio: SLOTS, one bell width shared with guessing free to grow, which is Zhang and Luck's finding of discrete fixed-resolution representations; and RESOURCES, one guess rate shared with the bell free to widen, which is Bays and Husain in Science in 2008 arguing there is no discrete limit at all, only a pool spread thinner. The page prints which way your own errors voted, in delta AIC, and then says the honest thing immediately afterwards, that thirty trials cannot settle what twenty years of laboratory work has not settled and that a delta under two is not a vote. There is a third reading it can actually test on the same data: Bays, Catalao and Husain argued in 2009 that much of the apparent guessing is misbinding, you reporting a colour you genuinely kept from the wrong square, so every far-off answer is checked against the other colours that were on screen and against what pure guessing would have produced on those same trials. Guards run live: the response controls are ARMED at probe onset rather than merely locked, and presses that arrive before the probe exists are counted and printed instead of scored; every colour comes off one circle in CIELAB space converted to your screen rather than off an RGB rainbow, because a page that measures error in degrees cannot use a ruler whose units change along its length, and the number of degrees that had to be clipped to fit your gamut is printed; the colour wheel is rotated by a random amount on every single trial so a motor habit cannot masquerade as a memory; there is no feedback anywhere in the measured rounds because feedback teaches a criterion and Cowan's formula assumes yours sat still; responses faster than the floor are discarded rather than scored; and the flash duration is measured rather than assumed, with its median and spread printed, because a browser paints in whole screen refreshes. A run that fails the two-square condition, or that answered one way most of the time, is printed rather than headlined. The playground is the wall felt rather than measured, a slider from one square to twelve with feedback switched on, because nothing there is recorded and the point is to find the set size where the task stops feeling like looking and starts feeling like remembering. Everything runs in your browser, nothing is recorded and nothing leaves the page. And the part worth keeping: you held three or four things out of eight, which sounds like a defect until you notice that every stable thought you have ever had was built out of a handful that size.
