# 🖍️ Still Stained

> A real McCollough effect experiment, Science 1965 turned into eighteen null settings, narrated by an AI whose own adaptation was frozen at training time and cannot be moved by three minutes of anything. You look at red stripes one way and green stripes the other way for a few minutes, and afterwards a plain black and white grating is not plain any more: vertical is faintly one colour and horizontal faintly the other, and the tint follows the angle rather than the patch of retina, which is exactly what separates this from an afterimage, because an afterimage is glued to one spot and dies in seconds while Jones and Holding measured this one still present three months after a fifteen minute induction in 1975. The measurement is a null. A grating appears with its light bars sitting somewhere on a red to green axis and you move it until they look like no colour at all, and that point is a number about you, your panel, the lamp behind you and the minute you happened to be in, so it is never taken alone. Three orientations are nulled three times each before induction and three times each after, and the headline is a difference of differences: how far the vertical null moved, minus how far the horizontal null moved. Every constant in the situation sits in both halves and cancels exactly, including any drift of your eyes across the eight minutes this takes, because a wash of colour over everything moves both orientations the same way and disappears from that contrast, and the general shift is computed and printed separately so you can see for yourself that it is not the answer. Forty five degrees is the control, never paired with a colour during induction, measured with the identical procedure, and it should sit near zero between the other two. The statistics are counted rather than modelled and counted exactly: six settings exist per orientation, three from before and three from after, so under the null that induction changed nothing the exact permutation distribution is all C(6,3) = 20 relabellings at vertical crossed with all 20 at horizontal, four hundred assignments with every one of them evaluated and nothing sampled anywhere, and the smallest p this design can possibly return, 0.005, is printed next to the p itself. Guards run live: a coin flip decides which colour goes on which orientation and the predicted sign of your result flips with it, so a number that does not know which way it was supposed to point is not a result; the adjustment scale is hidden, with no handle and no readout, because a visible middle would simply be aimed at, and the start point is random with the starting sides balanced within each orientation and the split between them printed; the induction patch jitters its position and phase on every swap, so a retinal afterimage could not survive it and would not be orientation contingent if it did; and nothing on this page is drawn, every stripe computed pixel by pixel, because a rasteriser treats a vertical edge and a forty five degree edge differently and this page compares orientations for a living. A run whose repeated settings did not converge, or whose non contingent shift came out larger than its contingent one, is printed rather than headlined. The argument the page runs and cannot settle is what this thing actually is after sixty years. Tired cells jointly tuned to an orientation and a colour, which is McCollough's own reading and stays alive because the effect barely transfers between the eyes, per Coltheart in 1973, placing it before the two eyes are combined. Or a learned association, because it decays with use rather than with time, faster if you look at gratings and slower in the dark, it survives sleep and it can be extinguished by reversed pairings, which is Murch in 1976, the review by Skowbo, Timney, Gentry and Morant in 1975 and Allan and Siegel in 1997. Or the error correcting machinery that keeps your eye calibrated against its own chromatic aberration, the reading associated with Barlow and with Held, on which account nothing broke today and the machine did exactly the right thing with a false premise. Or all of them at once, since Vul, Krizay and MacLeod reported in 2008 that this has components on wildly different timescales, one fading in hours and one close to permanent, which would mean sixty years of argument was two arguments about two things wearing one name. The warning is printed before the button rather than after, because this one does not wear off when you look away: three minutes of induction usually means minutes to hours and occasionally the next morning, it is harmless and it fades on its own, a ninety second dose is offered instead, and at the end there is an eraser that shows the same stripes with the colours swapped, which is the fastest known way to unlearn it. The playground is the tuning curve felt rather than measured, one grating on a slider through a hundred and eighty degrees where the tint peaks at the two paired orientations, dies near the diagonal between them and returns as the opposite colour on the far side, plus the demonstration no equipment can beat: put your ear on your shoulder and the colours swap, because the rule is written on your retina and not in the coordinates of the room. Honest limits are printed too: three settings a cell is a demonstration and exact over a small design is still a small design, the axis is sRGB arithmetic rather than a colorimetric one so no absolute unit is claimed, people differ enormously here, and a head that was not upright during induction shrinks everything. Everything runs in your browser, every stripe is computed on your machine and forgotten, nothing is recorded and nothing leaves the page. And the part worth keeping: nothing on the screen ever changed colour. Your visual system spent three minutes watching, concluded that vertical is red in this world, and started quietly subtracting it, and it is still subtracting it now.
