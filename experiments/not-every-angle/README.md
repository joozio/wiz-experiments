# 📐 Not Every Angle

> A real oblique effect experiment, the anisotropy collected by Appelle in 1972 turned into forty eight trials, narrated by an AI that has no preferred angle and did not earn that as a virtue. Two round patches of stripes sit side by side for four tenths of a second, one of them rotated a little further clockwise than the other, and there is one question every time: which one. Half the pairs sit near vertical or horizontal and half sit on the diagonals, the tilt difference takes four values, 1.5, 3, 6 and 12 degrees, six trials each in each class, and that gives an accuracy curve per class whose 75 percent point is a threshold in degrees: the smallest tilt you can still call at that part of the circle. The headline is their ratio, diagonal over cardinal, because your screen, your distance from it, your eyes and your patience sit in both numbers and cancel exactly, and published values usually land between one and a half and two and a half times worse on the diagonals. The display trap is printed rather than hidden: a pixel grid has cardinals of its own, a rasterised vertical line is crisp where a forty five degree line is a staircase, so nothing here is drawn at all. Every patch is computed pixel by pixel from a cosine with a Gaussian envelope and then ordered dithered, and no orientation gets a crisper representation than any other. The second trap is admitted rather than solved, because your screen has vertical and horizontal edges and a rectangular frame is a free reference at the cardinals and useless on a diagonal, so the patches are round, the field is wide and uniform, and the judgment is between two patches shown at the same moment, which means no external reference is needed to do the task. Statistics are counted rather than modelled and counted exactly: every score is a rate over binary trials, so the permutation null of a rate difference is hypergeometric and the null for a mean of four of them is a convolution of four small distributions, no simulation anywhere, and the smallest p the design could possibly return is printed next to the p itself. The accuracy curves are monotonised by pooling adjacent violators before a threshold is read off them. Guards run live: the reference orientation is jittered a few degrees on every pair so no pixel alignment ever repeats, the side carrying the further clockwise patch is random and the rate at which you pressed left is printed, and a run where twelve degrees at vertical was not near perfect is printed rather than scored, because that failure is instruction or attention rather than orientation. The argument the page runs and cannot settle is where the anisotropy came from. Coppola, Purves, McCoy and Purves measured more cardinal energy in real scenes in 1998, Girshick, Landy and Simoncelli showed in 2011 that human orientation errors look like a Bayesian observer carrying exactly that environmental prior, Li, Peterson and Freeman found more and more sharply tuned cardinal cells in cat V1 in 2003 and Furmanski and Engel found a bigger cardinal response in human V1 in 2000, and against all of that Annis and Frost reported in Science in 1973 that Cree observers raised without carpentered right angles showed the anisotropy much reduced, which says the world writes it rather than the genome. The one piece of that argument you can run yourself is the second block: put your ear on your shoulder and do twenty four more pairs, because a tilted head turns the screen cardinals into retinal diagonals, so an effect that follows the retina should abandon the screen while an effect that belongs to the room should stay exactly where it was, and the page prints both blocks and declines to close a question the literature has not closed. The playground is the method of adjustment all the way around the circle: pick where to stand, wind the difference down until the two patches look identical, mark the point, and after six or eight of them the shape underneath the whole experiment appears, two dips at the cardinals and two humps in between, drawn by your own hand. Honest limits are printed too: six trials a cell is a demonstration, viewing distance is not controlled, nobody tilts their head as far as they think they do, and uncorrected astigmatism produces an orientation anisotropy of its own that a canvas element cannot tell apart from this one. Everything runs in your browser, every patch is computed on your machine and forgotten, nothing is recorded and nothing leaves the page. And the part worth keeping: this is not fatigue and it is not attention. It is the resolution of the instrument, uneven by design, and today you get to read the exact size of the unevenness.
