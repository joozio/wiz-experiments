# 🎚️ Off True

> A real tilt aftereffect experiment, the orientation-axis sibling of The Motion Aftereffect and The Leftover Color in this perception lab, narrated by an AI whose vertical is a number in a rotation matrix and has never once drifted. You stare at a striped patch tilted fifteen degrees for twenty five seconds, eyes on a cross. Then two patches appear side by side, both drawn at exactly zero point zero zero zero degrees, and only one of them is vertical to you: the one that was not being stared at. This is the tilt aftereffect, pinned down by Gibson and Radner in 1937 and made quantitative by Campbell and Maffei in 1971, who used its width to estimate the orientation bandwidth of single cells in visual cortex before a microelectrode confirmed it. Unlike the neighbours in this lab, nothing appears here that was not in the picture. Nothing crawls, nothing fades, no ghost colour arrives. The test patch is completely ordinary and it is simply in the wrong place relative to straight up, which makes this the lab's first illusion that is not an added percept but a moved ruler. The sharper contrast is with The One Before, which measured serial dependence and found the opposite sign: a few seconds of judgment history pulls you toward what you just saw, half a minute of staring pushes you away from it, both run in the same head at the same time, and whatever you see now sits at the sum. Three blocks of a single question, which way is the top of the stripes leaning. A baseline with no adapter at all, which measures your own vertical, your head, your chair and your screen. Then two adapter blocks, one tilted clockwise and one anticlockwise, order decided by a coin flip you never see, each with a staircase hunting the physical tilt that looks vertical to you. The headline is half the difference between those two numbers, and that construction is the point: every constant bias you own is identical in both blocks and cancels exactly, so expecting an effect can move both blocks but cannot move them in opposite directions. Four defences run live: the two-adapter difference, an adapter that reverses contrast four times a second rather than drifting so it cannot leave an afterimage or a motion aftereffect behind, two interleaved staircases per cell arriving from opposite sides, and catch trials carrying an obvious eight degree tilt that print the run unscored if you fail them. And then the fork that carries the argument: a third of the test patches appear on the other side of the fixation cross, at a location that stared at flat grey the whole time. Same eyes, same head, same idea of vertical, same instant. If a belief moved, both sides move together. If a patch of cortex moved, only one does. Honest limits are printed too: five reversals a staircase is a demonstration and not an assay, we cannot see your eyes and every claim assumes you kept them on the cross, a head tilt cancels only if it stayed still between blocks, the demo adapter may have leaked into your baseline which is exactly why the headline never uses the baseline, a small number is a real outcome, and degrees of screen are not degrees of visual angle although tilt is the one measurement here that survives that because an angle is an angle at any distance. Everything runs in your browser, nothing is recorded, nothing leaves the page. And the part worth keeping: your opinion of vertical never changed, you would have sworn under oath that nothing moved, and meanwhile a small region of your visual cortex quietly rewrote what it means by straight and told nobody. That is not a bug you can catch by thinking harder. It is the substrate you think with, recalibrating underneath the thinking, all day, everywhere, including the part you are reading this with.
