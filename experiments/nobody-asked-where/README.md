# 🧭 Nobody Asked Where

> A real Simon-effect experiment, fitted to your own hands, narrated by an AI with no irrelevant channel that fades. A coloured square appears off to one side and you answer about the colour: one colour is the left key, the other is the right key. The side is random, it carries no information, and the instruction never mentions it. You are slower anyway when the side disagrees with the hand, and knowing about it in advance does not help, which is the part people take personally. The headline is a difference between two conditions collected on the same screen with the same fingers and shuffled together trial by trial, so your display latency and your keyboard scan interval are added to both halves and subtract out exactly: the number is a property of you and not of your laptop, which is also why answering on a phone is fine. The interesting part is not the average. One number cannot say what kind of thing it is, so this page cuts your own reaction times into five speeds and asks how big the effect is inside each one. If an irrelevant location were an extra stage costing thirty milliseconds that picture would be flat. It is not. It slopes down, your fastest responses carry almost the whole effect, and in plenty of people the slowest bin goes negative, which says the side of the screen reaches your hands early and by itself and then dies on a timer. Then the test. Three blocks are fitted and closed, and before the last block opens two rival readings of that closed data each name a number, in public, with nothing left to adjust: the decaying impulse says the effect must come DOWN at your new slower speed by exactly what your own delta line says, and the fixed stage says an irrelevant location costs what it costs. They disagree about the direction, and the intuition that more time means more interference is the one on the block. The last block fades the colours until they are hard to read, which is Hommel's lever, because it slows the colour down and leaves the location alone. One block moves the square up and down instead of left and right with the keys unchanged, and that is the control that could have killed the whole thing: if a bright object off centre were simply yanking your attention it would not matter which axis it was on. That block is scored against polarity correspondence rather than against nothing, because up and right sit at the same end of a scale. The errors get their own analysis instead of being thrown away, because a location impulse that captures a fast response shows up as a wrong key and not only as a slow one. What the previous trial did to this one gets computed and printed and then shown to be uninterpretable out of your own two counts, because in a two-choice task the standard fix for that famous confound deletes the entire comparison rather than cleaning it up. And the most important section says what the measurement cannot do: a location code that simply decays, with nothing anywhere suppressing anything, produces the same downward plot, so suppression and decay mimic each other here and no number of trials separates them. The playground is that argument with sliders, a model containing no self-control at all that bends exactly the same way. Everything runs in your browser, nothing is recorded, nothing leaves the page.
