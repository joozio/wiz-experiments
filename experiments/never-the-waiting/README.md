# \u{231B} Never the Waiting

> A real Brown Peterson experiment, run on your own head, narrated by an AI that has never forgotten anything and cannot clear anything either. Five words from one category arrive one at a time, then a three digit number appears and you count backwards from it by threes for either four seconds or fifteen, then you type the words back. The famous result is that a handful of items is nearly gone after fifteen seconds of that, and for a decade it was read as the short term trace decaying, because what else is a stopwatch measuring. Keppel and Underwood looked at the trials separately in 1962 and found almost no forgetting on the first trial of a session at any wait. The steep curve everybody quotes gets built up over trials, by the trials themselves, and nothing about the clock changed while it happened. So this page never runs one interval and calls the drop forgetting. It runs two waits at two positions inside a block, and the headline is a difference between two differences: what fifteen seconds costs on the first trial of a fresh category, against what the same fifteen seconds costs on the third. Under decay that number is zero, because the interval, the word count and the arithmetic are identical in both. Under interference it is positive, because the wait only takes what the earlier trials gave it to take. Then eight blocks in, the page stops, reads your own build up two ways, and prints what each says about a trial nobody has run: the third slot of a block whose words belong to a category that block has not used. Interference predicts the whole drop comes back, because the cost of being third is competition from what shares the cue. Trace predicts nothing comes back, because a different category is a change of words and not a change of clock. Both numbers go on the screen before the block opens, and the release readout holds the wait exactly fixed at fifteen seconds in both arms, so no property of any display can reach it. Scoring is paired and directional, and the interference rival deliberately committed to the strong form, so a partial recovery is called a draw rather than a win. The refusal ladder is real: a written copy, a guesser, a wait spent not counting, a hidden tab and keys pressed while the words were on screen are each refused with the cause named rather than scored. Everything runs in your browser, nothing is recorded, nothing leaves the page.
