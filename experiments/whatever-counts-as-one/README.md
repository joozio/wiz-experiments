# \u{1F9F1} Whatever Counts As One

> A real memory-span experiment, fitted to your own head, narrated by an AI whose own limit genuinely is counted in bits. A short list arrives one item at a time and you give it back in order, and the lists are made of four different things: ten decimal digits at 3.32 bits each, binary digits at one bit each, ten four-syllable words at 3.32 bits each, and the same binary digits arriving two at a time. If the limit were a fixed amount of information, a binary list would come back three and a third times longer than a decimal one. It does not. Miller said it as plainly as anyone ever has in 1956: bits are what stay constant in absolute judgment and CHUNKS are what stay constant in immediate memory, which leaves the question this page is built to put a number on. What counts as one thing? So halfway through you spend ninety seconds learning four pairs, 00 is 0 and 01 is 1 and 10 is 2 and 11 is 3, and then the binary lists come back in pairs exactly as they were in the block before and you give them back in binary exactly as you did before. Nothing about the screen or the keys changes; the only thing that changed is inside your head. Two rivals put a number on the screen before that block opens, both read off your own earlier blocks and both carrying their own intervals: the bits ceiling says recoding cannot manufacture information, so your span is the binary span you already had, and the chunk ceiling says a chunk now holds two digits, so your span is twice your digit span. The block is scored by which interval it clears rather than which number it lands nearer. The control that could kill it runs in the middle rather than living in a footnote: the same paired binary with no mapping taught, which carries the grouping and the practice and not the recoding, so the strong test is recoded against grouped rather than against raw binary. A second control is allowed to dent the headline, because ten long words carry the same bits and the same chunks as ten digits and still come back shorter, and the page measures that on the same evening and prints it against its own claim. No clock is read anywhere: a span is a count, not a latency, and a pair gets exactly two presentation slots, so a paired list and an unpaired one of the same length take exactly the same time to arrive. Every list is placed by a posterior over your span rather than by a staircase, which simulation showed to be the difference between an interval two items wide and one eight items wide. Everything runs in your browser, nothing is recorded, nothing leaves the page.
