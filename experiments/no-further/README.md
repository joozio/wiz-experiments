# 🎯 No Further

> A real Fitts law experiment, the 1954 original rebuilt as two crossing ladders and fitted to your own hand, narrated by an AI that reaches a target by writing its coordinates and therefore pays nothing to reach anything. The law is arithmetic: movement time equals a plus b times log2 of distance over width plus one, in the Shannon form MacKenzie gave it in 1992. Read what that says. Distance and width never appear separately, only as a ratio, so a target four times further away and four times wider takes exactly the same time to hit. Not roughly. The same. The distance is free and what you pay for is precision, precision is distance measured in target widths, and the unit of the answer is a bit, because Fitts was not measuring a speed, he was measuring a channel capacity: four to ten bits per second, through every input device anyone has built since. The design is two ladders that cross. Four blocks hold the target width fixed and walk the distance apart. Three hold the distance fixed and walk the width. Both climb through the same difficulty values from opposite directions, and every matched pair is an exact geometric scaling of its partner, the same picture four times bigger. The headline needs no model at all, just three measured averages: a baseline, a movement four times longer at the same width, and a movement four times longer at four times the width. Both arms travelled the same extra distance. Only one of them costs anything, and the difference carries an exact permutation test over the real intervals rather than a fitted curve. The model layer is then held to a harder standard than a fit: the line is fitted to the distance ladder only, four blocks where the bars never changed width, and then made to predict the width ladder it has never seen with no free parameters left to spend. A slope learned from distance predicting times produced by width is the entire content of the claim that only the ratio matters, and it is allowed to fail on the page. The calibration honesty here is a theorem rather than a disclaimer, twice over. Every number is an interval between two of your own taps on one clock, so a constant input lag adds the same amount to both timestamps and cancels exactly rather than folding into a parameter. And the index of difficulty is dimensionless, since distance and width are lengths in the same units, so it is the same number on a phone and on a 34 inch monitor: the first page in this perception lab whose x axis never had a unit to calibrate. What is not free is said out loud, because physical amplitude decides whether fingers, wrist or shoulder did the work and the price per bit differs between them, which is exactly why running it again on another device measures the bandwidth that device costs you. Speed and accuracy trade, so anyone can flatten a slope by hurrying and missing more, and the ISO 9241-9 answer is printed beside it: effective width, 4.133 times the spread of where your taps actually landed, the target you were really hitting, and throughput in bits per second, the number that survives a change of strategy. Guards run live: block order shuffled per run and printed, an unscored warm-up block absorbing the steep part of motor learning, the first three intervals of every block dropped, intervals under 80 ms discarded as impossible for a hand crossing a real gap, a tap landing nearer the wrong bar killing its interval as a mis-click rather than an aimed miss, a block over 35 percent errors refused with the words you were sweeping and not aiming, a whole run over that rate refused outright because the effective-width correction assumes landings scattered around a target and quietly flatters taps that were aimed at nothing, a run with too few surviving distance blocks refused a slope and named an absent measurement, a slope interval covering zero reported as exactly that with both ways it happens spelled out, an arena under 300 px refusing to run rather than quietly flooring the smallest target into a different experiment with the same name, and the timestamp resolution of your own browser measured from your own data and refused above 40 ms, because a browser coarsening its clock against fingerprinting will quantise every interval here. The playground draws the same ratio at two sizes so you can see two completely different pictures carry one identical number. And the part worth keeping is what it explains about every screen you have ever used: the edge is an infinitely wide target because you cannot overshoot past it, which is why the menu bar lives at the very top and why a corner is the fastest place on any display, a dock that magnifies under the cursor is buying width, and a menu that opens under your hand has just set the distance to zero. None of that is decoration. It is somebody spending your four to ten bits per second more carefully. Everything runs in your browser, nothing is recorded, nothing leaves the page.
