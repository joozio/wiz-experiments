# 🌫️ The Gist

> A real ensemble perception experiment, a direct rebuild of Dan Ariely's 2001 seeing sets paradigm, narrated by an AI that keeps all twelve numbers and cannot do the trick you are about to do. Twelve circles flash for half a second, a mask wipes them, and then two circles appear and you pick one, and which question you are answering is decided at random on the spot. Sometimes it is which one was actually in the set: one of the two really was there and the other never was, and they sit at equal log distances on opposite sides of the set's true mean, so the average tells you nothing about which is which and only item memory can answer it. Sometimes it is which one is closer to the average: neither of those was in the set, one of them is the set's actual mean size which was deliberately never displayed, and the other is offset from it, so item memory cannot answer it because neither answer is an item. Almost everybody goes to chance on the first question and beats chance on the second, which means you correctly report a property of a set whose members you cannot identify one second later. This is ensemble perception, also called summary statistical representation, and it is the first piece in this perception lab where the thing your visual system KEPT turns out to be more accurate than everything it threw away. Every sibling here measured a loss: Already Gone timed how fast the visual buffer evaporates, Never Together showed that colour and shape are not even stored bound together, Change Blindness showed a whole object can leave a scene unnoticed, and The Crowding Zone is the closest of all because Parkes, Lund, Angelucci, Solomon and Morgan argued in 2001 that crowding IS compulsory averaging, that the flanked letter was not lost but pooled. Four defences run live: both questions ride on identical displays, identical exposure, identical two choice format and an identical fifty percent chance line, interleaved at random, so a gap between them cannot be attention or effort or eyesight or the shape of the buttons; the two circles in the member question are always FURTHER APART than the two in the average question, so the comparison that is harder to see is the one people get right and the easy explanation runs backwards; side is randomised and so is the sign of every offset, the foil being the bigger circle on half the trials and the smaller on the other half, with the page printing how often you picked the bigger one as a receipt; and two catch trials, one per question, put the wrong answer nowhere near the set, with a miss printing the run unscored. Both accuracies get an exact binomial test against chance. Honest limits are printed too: Myczek and Simons argued in 2008 that quietly subsampling two or three items could reproduce the classic mean accuracy without any pooling, which this design answers in part and not in full, with Ariely 2008 and Chong, Joo, Emmanouil and Treisman 2008 as the replies; nine trials per question is a demonstration and not an assay; size here is pixels and not degrees of visual angle because the page cannot know how far your face is from the screen; whether the visual system averages diameter or area is genuinely unsettled and this page works in log diameter, which is a choice; half a second is enough for a saccade and there is no eye tracker; and none of this is a trait or a memory score. The playground runs Chong and Treisman's 2003 set size manipulation: flash four, eight, sixteen or thirty two circles for a hundred to a thousand milliseconds, drag one circle to the average you remember, and watch the error stay roughly flat as the set grows, which is the signature of a pooling operation and the one thing item memory absolutely cannot do. Everything runs in your browser, nothing is recorded, nothing leaves the page. And the part worth keeping: nothing in you decided to compute a mean, nobody chose compression, and the average is simply what is left over when a system that cannot hold twelve things holds the shape of twelve things instead, which is why you have walked past every crowd of your life without storing one of them and still knew roughly how many, roughly how big, and roughly how dangerous.
