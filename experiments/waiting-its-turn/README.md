# 🚦 Waiting Its Turn

> A real psychological refractory period experiment, fitted to your own hands, narrated by an AI whose own queue is the whole of its architecture. The last two pages in this lab measured how expensive one decision is: the hand as a channel with a bit rate, then choosing as a channel with a bit rate. This one asks how many decisions you can make at the same time, and the answer is one. Not one at a time is faster. One. Somewhere between recognising a thing and deciding what to do about it there is a stage that will not run twice at once, and when a second job arrives while the first is inside it, the second job stands in a hallway and waits. Telford called it a refractory period in 1931, Welford gave it its shape in 1952, and Pashler spent two decades turning it into the most falsifiable claim in cognitive psychology. Two tasks: a tone, high or low, answered with the left hand, and a letter, X or O, answered with the right hand, arriving some head start after the tone. If the second task waits for the middle stage of the first, the waiting shrinks one millisecond for every millisecond of head start, so the line through the queueing region has a slope of exactly minus one. Not negative. Not roughly. Minus one, a number with no free parameters in it, printed with a bootstrap interval around it so you can see whether your own hands produced it. The elbow where the curve flattens is then fitted with that slope pinned, and compared against your reaction to the tone measured in a block that never contained a letter at all. And then the part that turns an effect into a dissection, because anyone can show that two jobs at once are slower than one and that is arithmetic about effort rather than a discovery. Make the letter hard to see. Drop its contrast until resolving it takes an extra fifty or eighty milliseconds. That cost is paid in front of the queue, and in the queueing region the algebra says the two terms cancel exactly, so the same faint letter that costs you its full price at a nine hundred millisecond head start costs you nothing whatsoever at fifty. Same letter, same eyes, same page: a difficulty that is real when the task is alone and free when the task is queued. It has an ugly beautiful name, underadditivity, and it is the headline here: three measured numbers, one of which is supposed to be zero. The control that decides whether any of it means anything is the tone task itself, regressed on head start and required to come out flat. The artifact that eats this experiment alive is response grouping, holding the first answer and firing both keys as one motor packet, so the gap between your two presses is measured on every trial and above forty percent grouping the headline is refused outright. Two clocks are involved and neither is trusted: the audible onset comes from the browser own output timestamp, the painted onset from the animation frame after the one that drew it, and every regression runs on the measured difference trial by trial rather than on the requested value. Whatever output latency stays unadmitted is a constant, and a constant cannot bend a slope, cannot survive a difference of differences, and cannot break the elbow comparison because both sides of it move together. Guards run live: a three tone sound check, unscored practice, solo blocks split before and after the dual blocks so practice drift cannot contaminate the yardstick, head start and contrast randomised trial by trial, thin cells refused, high error rates refused with the reason named, and the browser own clock quantum recovered from your data. Honest limits are printed rather than buried, from graded capacity sharing to the argument that the queue is a strategy rather than a structure. And the thing worth taking away is that this queue has been in you the whole time, in every sentence begun before the last one landed, and you have never once felt it. Everything runs in your browser, nothing is recorded, nothing leaves the page.
